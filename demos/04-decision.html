From numbers to a decision: drawing the line

Each dot is one lesion, with features extracted from the image in the network from our previous example. Imagine the classifier is a line. Everything on the red side is called "suspicious". Move the line with the sliders to alter the decision boundary, or let the computer fit it. This is a visual representation of the weights and biases that make the classifier, which are updated during model training to get the highest accuracy.

Each step checks every lesion against the current line, then nudges w₁, w₂ and b a little in the direction that reduces the error. Faint lines show where it has been.

Big steps get there faster but can overshoot. Tiny steps won't overshoot but take longer. Learning rate is a model parameter.
Score = w₁ × outline + w₂ × wiggles + b. A positive score means "suspicious". Squashing the score through an S-shaped curve (the sigmoid) turns it into a probability. "Training" just means nudging w₁, w₂ and b, over and over, to make fewer mistakes.
A real network does the same thing with hundreds of numbers instead of two, so the "line" becomes a surface in hundreds of dimensions. Nobody can plot that.
